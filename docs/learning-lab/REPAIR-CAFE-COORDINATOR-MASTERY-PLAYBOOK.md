# Run Your Own Repair Café — coordinator mastery playbook
**Version:** 11 October 2026 · **Module:** [Interactive event-day simulator](https://josh-hub-96no.vercel.app/learn/repair-cafe-simulator) · **Companion learning:** [AssetFlow Repair Café event course](https://dubbo-ewaste-app.vercel.app/learn/repair-cafe-running)

> **Status: substantial simulated practice, not independently certified competence.** All scenarios, names, venue permissions, numbers and outcomes in this module are fictional. Do not publish an event, assign a real volunteer, handle customer devices, or infer an insurance/licensing position from this course.

## Purpose, audience and practical boundary

The learning outcome is **a calm, accountable coordinator of a small, volunteer-led, repair-together event**, not someone who guarantees repairs or achieves the highest item count. Learners practise how to balance access, volunteer capacity, safety, ownership and realistic outcomes. They should finish able to explain *why* a decision was made, what assumptions they tested, which uncertainties remain, what changed as a result, and how they would improve the next event.

**Time guide:** 70–110 minutes total in short sessions:
1. 10–15 min: read source guidance, map scope, ownership and risk.
2. 15–20 min: select venue, adjust floor plan and build an *accepted* roster.
3. 20–30 min: run the event desk and record seven irreversible first decisions; address any simulated recovery, with consequences retained.
4. 15–20 min: compare one independent real-world example through a film, podcast and house-rules discussion.
5. 10–20 min: build, submit and export a specific final plan; schedule a review after seven days.

There is **no automatic publishing or use of actual Repair Café Dubbo database records**. Learner progress is browser-local. The Groq written assessment is only requested when the learner presses the grading button; avoid entering identifiable volunteer information, passwords, student records or confidential repair tickets.

## Pedagogical architecture

| Method | What the learner does | What counts as evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Worked example | Observe a plausible situation plus a professional explanation and limits | Learner can cite the relevant evidence rather than simply identify the 'nice' option |
| Visual/spatial learning | Select a venue and active repair stations on the illustrated floor plan | The activities match the written permitted scope and accessible route |
| Authentic workflow | Confirm specific accepted shifts, lead, welcome and skill coverage | Nobody is double-booked or treated as committed merely because they're interested |
| Consequence simulation | Choose once for seven live incidents | Persistent choice ledger, queue/team/trust changes and situation pressure based on earlier actions |
| Errorful learning | If unsafe, stop the fictional activity and document safe recovery | First mistake remains visible and costs team time and queue capacity |
| Deliberate retrieval | Return a week later and justify two previous decisions without looking | Specific alternative considered, evidence and revised decision |
| Transfer task | Produce a small, realistic next-month plan | Accountable role + measurable outcome + explicit safety/consent boundary |
| Human observation | If authorised, shadow an experienced local coordinator at a genuine event | Supervisor witness and dated feedback, never claimed by the simulator |

**Do not use a high completion percentage as evidence of workplace capability.** An AI rubric also cannot independently observe someone's event leadership or check actual insurance/venue approvals.

## Working model: an event has more than one 'queue'

Think about the event as five connected flows. A change at any point can cause a later problem:

```text
Venue approval + scope + cover
            |
Accepted lead + welcome + station shifts
            |
Visitor arrives → access + house rules + consent → queue
            |                                   |
Hazard/exclusion check                        Skilled station
            |                                   |
    Stop/refer if needed                Repair WITH owner
            |                                   |
      Owner retains item ← item handback + outcome notes
            |
        Close + debrief → one improvement → next event
```

**Key distinction:** venue availability, permission, volunteer availability, shift acceptance, safe competence, owner consent and data responsibility are **different facts**. One never automatically establishes another.

### Common local planning questions to resolve *outside* the simulator

- Who actually owns, manages and approves the chosen venue for the specific time, scope and group?
- Which activities are permitted, and who has reviewed the appropriate insurance and safety/incident arrangements?
- Which lead and welcome volunteers have *accepted specific shifts*? Are roles and relevant skills represented without double-booking?
- How will accessibility, accessible toilets/routes, heat, waiting chairs, signage, first aid and emergency exits be checked with the actual venue?
- How will owner consent, custody, photo consent, privacy/data access, waiting times and safe referrals be handled with minimum required data?
- What items are **out of scope** (including unapproved mains electrical work, damaged lithium devices and potentially hazardous equipment), and how will a volunteer stop without feeling pressured to 'have a go'?
- What happens if the host changes access or two volunteers cancel in the last 48 hours?

## Four deeply worked cases

### Case 1 — You may have volunteers but not a venue

**Evidence presented:** The library expresses interest in a Saturday morning. The room is provisionally free, but the host hasn't confirmed the insurance/activity scope. Three fixers have accepted shifts; the lead is only 'available'. A draft flyer says the event is confirmed and offers sewing, laptop support and bicycles.

**Best-supported response:** Do not publish that flyer. Seek written venue/date/activity approval, confirm actual lead acceptance and all essential role coverage, scope the activities to those the host allows and check risk/cover. The responsible coordinator owns the decision to publish *only after* gates have been satisfied.

**Plausible alternatives and trade-offs:** A tentative 'save the date' without claiming a confirmed place may be useful for *internal planning* but should not be confused with an approved public invitation. Moving to an outdoor stall might raise visibility but adds weather, access and additional scope questions. No public date is more responsible than a misleading date.

**Mastery prompt:** Name three independent evidence items that would change your recommendation, and write two versions of a polite message (one to host, one to potential volunteers), without inventing that anything has been booked.

### Case 2 — The person handling intake disappears

**Evidence presented:** At 09:50, the confirmed welcome volunteer reports a delay. Five people are waiting. The event lead is occupied with risk checks, and a trained computer fixer is already managing the computer station.

**Best-supported response:** Stop or slow new check-ins rather than creating an unregistered waiting group. Make a documented, short-term role handover to a *competent, available* welcome volunteer or reduce scope. State realistic wait times and ensure everyone knows where to queue safely.

**Competing demands:** Asking the computer fixer to cover both roles may appear efficient but abandons the station. A temporary simplified intake may be defensible *only if* its essential hazard, consent and ownership requirements remain intact; do not leave important controls to be completed after work has begun.

**Mastery prompt:** Draw the revised roster and write what you would tell a frustrated visitor. Describe the trigger for re-opening the queue.

### Case 3 — An unexpected hazardous item and a request to leave a laptop behind

**Evidence presented:** A kettle with a damaged mains lead is presented to a pop-up with no qualified electrical testing/repair station. A separate visitor asks a fixer to keep their laptop and password while they run an errand. The waiting queue is now seven.

**Best-supported response:** Do not energise or take custody of the unsafe kettle for unapproved work. Explain a competent repair/referral path and owner retention. Do not record the laptop password or accept unattended custody; offer an owner-present appointment or a suitable professional referral. Record the decisions and communicate wait limitations without promising a fix.

**Plausible alternatives:** A volunteer might suggest a visual assessment of the kettle; however, a documented hazard and unapproved station call for a scope-based referral rather than implying clearance. Offering private password storage might feel helpful but creates avoidable risk.

**Mastery prompt:** Write one minute of calm, respectful verbal explanation to each visitor. Identify which facts you would *not* record in a public queue.

### Case 4 — Reporting honest outcomes when repairs are not guaranteed

**Fictional case counts:** 17 visitors arrived; 4 items were repaired, 6 were diagnosed/referred, 3 were safely declined, and 4 visitors were still waiting at closing.

**Best-supported response:** These are **not** 17 proven waste diversions. Record repair and diagnosis as separate statuses. Clarify the disposition of the four waiting items, and ensure handover to owners. Any privacy-sensitive record must stay within the approved system and consent. Do not infer environmental impact from a repair attempt.

**Transfer plan:** Choose one change: for example, a clearly communicated final check-in time or a named backup intake volunteer. State a responsible role and a measure such as people waiting at closing, estimated wait accuracy or a simple anonymous post-event visitor survey. Compare at least two subsequent events before promising effectiveness.

**Mastery prompt:** Prepare a 150-word board update stating verified findings, unknowns, one improvement and why another tempting metric is misleading.

## A coordinator's evidence portfolio

Use the simulation's export once a passing written debrief is recorded. A complete training portfolio contains:

1. Venue/scope decision and specific restrictions.
2. Accepted roster, accountable lead, welcome coverage and station competencies.
3. Four readiness gates and three reasoning checks passed.
4. Seven first incident choices with optional original field notes, consequences and recovery records.
5. A brief final improvement plan with **the incident it addresses, a credible alternative, the proposed action, accountable role and measurable evidence of success**.
6. Optional media reflections and source citations; real event observation must be recorded separately.

**Strong reasoning is not just a long answer.** It uses the facts present, distinguishes recorded fact from assumption, compares defensible alternatives, explains who can approve the action and names the safe stop condition. A 24-word response may be rejected by AI if it lacks those features; a long response can also fail.

## Assessment rubric — human reviewer or learner self-check

Score each criterion from **0 (not demonstrated) to 4 (excellent)**:

| Dimension | At 1/4 | At 3/4 | At 4/4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Understanding | Lists a plausible action but misses the operational question | Recommends a justified action under correct scope | Explicitly connects people, service and authority and handles exceptions |
| Evidence | Repeats a general rule | Uses specific facts from the incident and known constraints | Tests uncertainty and states what new information could reverse the decision |
| Alternatives | Says 'the other answer is wrong' | Compares a genuinely plausible option with trade-offs | Explains the opportunity cost and conditions where the alternative wins |
| Safety/uncertainty | Assumes consent or risk controls | Names stop conditions, consent and limits | Separates immediate mitigation, accountability, recordkeeping and follow-up |

**Suggested simulated mastery threshold:** 12/16 or higher, with understanding and evidence at least 3 each, alternatives and safety/uncertainty at least 2, **plus no unresolved incident recovery**. This is aligned with JoshHub's server-side Groq rubric. It is **not** an endorsed occupational certification; the model is not a substitute for a qualified reviewer.

### What an observed competence claim would additionally require

At an actual approved event (with no learner-created risk), an authorised coordinator would observe the learner completing a welcome/triage handover, reading the real site risk plan, communicating a fair wait-time boundary and closing a fictional or routine low-risk item with its owner. The observer should record the date, scope, behaviours witnessed, concerns and a next action. No simulated grade alone qualifies a person to supervise hazardous or regulated electrical work.

## Media study cards (all original sources linked)

**Watch (10–15 min):** [Repair Café International documentary](https://www.repaircafe.org/en/anniversary-documentary-on-15-years-of-repair-cafe/) (2024; publisher states optional English subtitles). Notice whether the visitor learns alongside a volunteer and how the event makes repair approachable. Explain one technique that transfers to a small Dubbo pilot. External media is not rehosted or represented as a new video.

**Listen (15–30 min):** [Restart Radio](https://therestartproject.org/podcast/) — choose a public episode about community repair, organising or maintenance. Record what the episode actually said, and distinguish that from your own proposed staffing rule. Podcast length and transcript availability differ between episodes.

**Read:** [Repair Café house rules](https://www.repaircafe.org/en/house-rules/) emphasise voluntary repair-together activities, possible refusal, owner responsibility for unrepaired objects and a one-item-first queue. They are **international templates**; they cannot eliminate non-excludable local legal duties.

**Apply official guidance:** The [NSW Government Event Starter Guide](https://www.nsw.gov.au/departments-and-agencies/premiers-department/community-engagement/event-starter-guide/risk-assessment-management) calls for risk identification, impact/likelihood analysis, control measures, continuous monitoring, clear communication and records. Draft a venue-specific risk register for a hypothetical indoor event and identify what remains to verify before it could be used in real life.

## Seven-day review

Without reopening the answer key, revisit: (a) a delayed welcome shift, (b) unapproved mains work, and (c) a queue that exceeds capacity. For each, state the initial facts, your best response, the next piece of evidence needed and an alternative you rejected. Compare against the event ledger, then revise the next-month improvement plan if necessary.

## Release QA and known limitations

- Automated logic tests check that **first choices cannot be rewritten**, wrong choices impose consequences, event progression requires safe recovery and no final certification is awarded from an incomplete ledger or an unassessed reflection.
- GitHub / Vercel builds establish compile and unit-test status, **not actual Android usability, screen-reader success or independent learner performance**.
- This module now has more realistic *stateful consequence and context branching*, but it is **not a physics-level or real-world attendance simulator**. Trust, energy and queue are deliberately simple *illustrative indicators*, never empirical measurements.
- Real source links and their meaning have been checked against Repair Café International, NSW Government and The Restart Project on 11 October 2026. Media playback and captions may depend on the publisher and the learner's device.
- The AI grading pathway is susceptible to model error and sends the learner's submitted draft to Groq. No personally identifiable user submissions should be entered.
- Coordinator-approved human observation and a genuine Dubbo venue/volunteer/safety plan remain outstanding before claiming this learning transfers to practice.
